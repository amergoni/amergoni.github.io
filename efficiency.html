How efficient is a school?

Two schools can get the same results with very different budgets. Efficiency analysis asks how much each one could improve, by comparing it with the schools that do best with what they have. Drag the schools below and see what changes.

Frontier
Question

Efficiency scores

    Constant returns: a school twice as big should achieve twice as much. The frontier is the straight line through the school with the best results per euro.

    Variable returns: small and large schools may face different conditions. Schools are compared with similar-sized schools, or mixes of them.

    Free disposal hull: schools are only compared with real schools that do at least as well, never with mixes. The frontier becomes a staircase.

    1 · The frontier

    Best practice, drawn from the data

    The blue line connects the schools that achieve the most with their resources. Nobody has to decide in advance what a "good" school looks like: the best observed schools set the standard. This approach is called Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), because the frontier wraps around, or envelops, the data.

    2 · The score

    Distance to the frontier

    A school's score measures how far it is from the frontier. With the question "same results, less money?", a score of 80% means the school could reach its results with 80% of its budget. With "same money, better results?", it means the school achieves 80% of what its budget allows. Schools on the frontier score 100%.

    3 · The benchmarks

    Who to learn from

    The arrow points to the school's target on the frontier. The schools that define that part of the frontier are its benchmarks: comparable schools that show what is possible. Try moving one benchmark, and watch the scores of the others change.

    Why it matters for policy

    Governments spend large budgets on schools, courts and social protection, and they want to know whether that money is well used. Efficiency analysis gives a fair comparison: each unit is judged against similar units, not against an abstract ideal. In my research I combine it with causal methods, to ask not only whether a policy works, but whether it helps schools and services use their resources better. Examples include the effect of ICT on school efficiency and principals' management practices.

    Real analyses go further than this toy: they use several inputs and outputs at once, account for outliers with robust estimators, and compare units in similar contexts with conditional estimators. For a full overview of the field, see Fifty years of data envelopment analysis.

    Try it with your own data

    In R, the Benchmarking package computes the scores shown above, and my package rcDEA adds robust and conditional versions. This is the example from the chart: 

    library(Benchmarking)
library(rcDEA)

spending    <- c(5, 6.5, 8, 9.5, 7, 11, 12.5, 10)   # input, € thousand per pupil
proficiency <- c(52, 70, 76, 84, 55, 80, 90, 62)    # output, % proficient

# Standard DEA, variable returns to scale, input orientation
eff(dea(spending, proficiency, RTS = "vrs", ORIENTATION = "in"))

# Robust (order-m) DEA: less sensitive to outliers
r <- robust_DEA(input = spending, output = proficiency, m = 5, B = 200,
                RTS = "vrs", ORIENTATION = "in")
r$eff
